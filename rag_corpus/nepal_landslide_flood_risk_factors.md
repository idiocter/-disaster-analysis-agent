# Landslide and Flood Risk Factors in Nepal's Terai and Hill Regions

## Deforestation and slope stability

Loss of forest cover on sloped terrain reduces root-mass soil cohesion and increases
surface runoff velocity, both of which are established contributing factors to
landslide susceptibility in the Himalayan foothills. Municipalities that combine
recent forest loss with moderate-to-steep slopes (roughly 15-35 degrees, where soil
tends to be thick enough to fail catastrophically but the terrain is not so steep that
soil cannot accumulate at all) are generally considered higher-risk than either factor
alone would suggest.

## Rainfall intensity

Nepal's monsoon season (roughly June to September) delivers the large majority of
annual precipitation in short, intense bursts rather than evenly across the year.
Short-duration high-intensity rainfall is a stronger landslide trigger than the same
total volume spread over more days, because soil pore pressure builds faster than it
can drain. Annual rainfall totals are a coarse proxy for this dynamic -- they do not
capture intensity directly -- but in the absence of sub-daily precipitation data,
higher annual totals are still a reasonable directional signal for elevated risk in a
composite index.

## Terai-specific flood risk

The Terai lowlands, by contrast, are more exposed to river flooding than landslides
given their flat topography; upstream deforestation and rainfall intensity in the
hills feed downstream flood risk in Terai municipalities even when the Terai area
itself has low slope. A composite risk index built only from within-municipality
slope and local forest loss can therefore understate flood risk for low-lying
municipalities that are hydrologically downstream of degraded hill catchments -- a
known limitation worth stating explicitly rather than silently ignoring.

## On composite heuristic indices

Weighted composite indices (combining forest-loss percentage, slope, and rainfall
into a single score) are a common first-pass screening tool in disaster-risk
literature precisely because ground-truth incident data is often sparse or
unavailable at fine administrative granularity. Such indices should be presented as
indicative screening tools, not as validated predictive models, unless calibrated
against real historical incident records.
